Overview

What is Lenme?

Lenme is a peer-to-peer lending app that connects everyday people who need quick loans with everyday investors. Unlike traditional investing platforms that require large minimums or financial expertise, Lenme allows anyone to start investing with as little as $50.

I was brought in as the Product Designer for the investor side, responsible for the full experience — from discovering loan requests and evaluating borrowers to submitting offers and tracking repayments.

I worked closely with the PM and two engineers across three months, running short iteration cycles from research insights through low-fidelity wireframes, interactive prototypes, and final visual polish.

Scope clarification: This case study covers the investor-facing flow only. The borrower experience, onboarding, and payment processing were handled by separate team members. My primary focus was the marketplace, borrower evaluation, and offer submission screens.

Problem Space

Investors were leaving without acting.

Before starting any design work, I sat down with the PM to understand where the product was struggling. Analytics showed that investors were browsing the marketplace but converting to offers at a low rate. The drop-off was concentrated in two moments: evaluating a borrower's profile, and completing the offer form.

The existing app had the data — but presenting financial data ≠ building investor confidence. The core tension was: how do you make a risky financial decision feel manageable?

What the app had

✗ Raw credit data with no interpretation or context
✗ Offer form with 6+ manual inputs and no defaults
✗ No visual hierarchy — everything felt equally important
✗ No feedback after offer submission — total silence
✗ Portfolio view was fragmented across multiple tabs

What investors needed

✓ A simple trust signal that summarizes borrower risk
✓ Smart defaults that reduce cognitive load at decision time
✓ Clear visual priority — "what matters most right now?"
✓ Status visibility after submitting an offer
✓ A single view of their full investment activity

Research

What I did to understand users.

I ran a lean research sprint over two weeks before touching any wireframes. The goal was simple: understand what prevents everyday people from investing on Lenme — and what makes them trust it enough to act.

🎙️
User Interviews
One-on-one remote sessions exploring mental models around investing, risk, and trust. I recruited users who had signed up for Lenme but never funded a loan.

Reviewed drop-off data from Mixpanel with the PM to identify where users were abandoning the flow. Cross-referenced with support ticket themes.

Limitation I'll be upfront about: Six interviews is a small sample, and all participants were recruited from Lenme's existing user base — so they already had some platform familiarity. The findings are directional, not statistically conclusive. They were useful enough to prioritize focus areas and validate with testing later.

Affinity mapping — what I found

After synthesizing the interview notes, I grouped recurring themes into patterns. Three clusters emerged clearly across multiple participants:

01

Raw data creates anxiety, not confidence

Participants could see credit scores and DTI ratios, but didn't know how to interpret them. Seeing a "620 credit score" without context made them feel less equipped to decide, not more. They wanted someone to just tell them: is this a safe bet or not?

"I see all these numbers but I don't know what's good or bad. Like, is 620 good?"

→ Design implication: Build a single composite trust signal (Lenme Score) that interprets the data for them.

02

The offer form was the biggest point of paralysis

5 of 6 usability sessions saw users slow down or abandon the flow entirely at the offer form. Having to manually input interest rate felt like a test they could fail. "What if I pick the wrong rate and lose money?" was the dominant fear.

"I don't want to offer too low — that's dumb. But too high and they'll reject me. How do I know the right number?"

→ Design implication: Replace blank inputs with smart defaults based on borrower risk profile. Let users adjust, not start from zero.

03

Post-submission silence killed trust

Users who had previously submitted offers reported feeling "ghosted" — they had no idea if the offer was seen, pending, or dead. Several participants said they stopped checking the app because it felt pointless.

"I made an offer three weeks ago and I still don't know what happened to it."

→ Design implication: Build a clear pending offers view with real-time status and notifications.

Design Direction

Three principles that guided every decision.

Before wireframing anything, I defined three principles directly from the research. Every screen decision had to answer to at least one of these. This helped me push back on feature requests that didn't serve the core investor problem.

Principle 01
Interpret, don't just display
Raw financial data without context creates anxiety. The design should translate numbers into meaning — what does a 620 credit score actually tell me as an investor?
Principle 02
Reduce the blank page problem
Blank input fields force users to generate answers from nothing. Wherever possible, give a smart default that users adjust — not a blank they have to fill cold.
Principle 03
Status visibility at every step
Investing is stressful precisely because outcomes are uncertain. Reduce that stress by making system state visible — pending, active, completed — clearly at all times.

Ideation & Iteration

What I tried first — and why I changed it.

I went through two major rounds of wireframing before arriving at the final design. The early explorations helped me test assumptions quickly with the PM and one real user before investing time in high-fidelity screens.

Marketplace — V1 vs V2 How to present borrower listings
V1 — Information-heavy card
My first instinct was to show everything on the card: credit score, DTI, loan amount, duration, purpose, history. The idea was "more info = more informed decision."

After a quick prototype review with two participants, both said the card felt overwhelming. One said he didn't even know where to look first. The card had no hierarchy — everything competed equally for attention.
V2 — Signal-first card (final)
I stripped the card down to only what helps with a pass/fail decision at a glance: the Lenme Score, loan amount, duration, and avatar color. Detail is preserved but moved into the profile view — accessed on tap.

This reduced "browse → detail → back" friction significantly. Users could scan the list faster and tapped into profiles they were actually interested in — instead of trying to read every card fully.
Offer Form — Key Iteration From blank inputs to smart defaults
V1 — Blank form
The original form had three fields — amount, APR, and duration — all blank. This was the #1 drop-off point in our usability sessions. Participants froze. Even those who wanted to invest said they didn't know if their numbers were "correct."
V2 — Pre-filled defaults (final)
I worked with the PM to define a default APR range based on Lenme Score bands. The form now pre-fills a suggested APR and amount — within market norms for that risk profile. Users still have full control to adjust sliders, but they start from a reasonable baseline, not zero. This single change had the most visible impact on offer completion rate.

What I'd do differently: I wish I had run a formal A/B test on the V1 vs V2 marketplace card, not just informal reviews. I moved to V2 based on qualitative feedback from 2 people — which was directionally right but harder to defend with data. In future projects I want to build lightweight testing infrastructure earlier.

Design Solutions

Five touchpoints, one clear flow.

1

Curated Loan Marketplace

Each borrower card now leads with the Lenme Score — a composite trust signal — alongside loan amount, duration, and an avatar color coding risk at a glance. Secondary data like credit band and loan purpose is visible but visually subordinate. The layout allows investors to scan quickly and decide which borrowers to explore further.

Why: Research showed users were overwhelmed by equal-weight data. Leading with the Lenme Score gave them a "primary judgment" to react to first, then explore detail. Scan speed improved noticeably in follow-up testing.
2

Smart Filtering System

Filters organized around investor intent, not raw data fields: Risk Profile (Low / Medium / High), Credit Score Band, Loan Amount Range, Repayment Period, and Borrower History. The filter panel opens as a bottom sheet to maintain context — users don't lose the list they were browsing.

Why: Early wireframes had filters in a full-screen modal. Users lost orientation — they'd filter and come back to a list they didn't recognize. Bottom sheet preserved spatial context. Borrower History filter was added after one user asked "can I see people who've paid back before?" — that wasn't in the original spec.
3

Borrower Snapshot — Trust at a Glance

The borrower profile now answers "can I trust this person?" before anything else. The Lenme Score sits prominently at the top. Below it: a financial health summary (payment history, credit utilization, income verification) and a plain-language explanation of the key risk indicators. Raw data like DTI is still present but labeled with context ("Good: under 36%").

Why: The most common interview quote was some version of "I see the numbers but I don't know what they mean." Labeling thresholds (Good / Fair / Concerning) directly on the data reduced that confusion without removing the underlying information for users who wanted it.
4

Submit Offer — Smart Defaults

The offer modal pre-fills a suggested APR based on the borrower's Lenme Score band. Amount and duration have sliders with clear min/max bounds. A projected return estimate updates in real time as users adjust inputs — so they can see the outcome of their choices before committing.

Why: The single biggest drop-off in the existing flow. Replacing blank inputs with smart defaults directly addressed the "decision paralysis" finding from research. The real-time return estimate also gave users a sense of what they stood to gain — making the action feel worthwhile, not just risky.
5

Portfolio & Offer Status Dashboard

A unified view of all investor activity: Pending → Accepted → Repaying → Completed → Default. Clear state labels, repayment progress bars, and ROI summaries are visible at a glance. Push notifications for status changes close the feedback loop that was causing users to disengage after submitting offers.

Why: Three interview participants said they stopped using the app because they never knew what happened to their offers. Making system state visible — and actively notifying users when it changes — converts passive drop-off into active re-engagement.

Reflection

What I'd do differently.

This project taught me a lot about designing for financial anxiety — and about the limits of small research samples. Here's my honest assessment of what worked, what I'd improve, and what I'm still curious about.

What worked well
Defining design principles from research before wireframing gave me a framework to evaluate every decision — and push back on scope creep.
The smart defaults on the offer form had the most direct, measurable impact. When in doubt, reduce blank inputs.
Mapping the borrower flow (not just the investor flow) helped me understand what data was actually available — and design around it instead of inventing features.
What I'd improve
I'd run formal usability tests on the final prototype before launch — not just internal reviews. One round of testing caught issues I hadn't noticed after weeks of being close to the work.
Six interviews was too small a sample to be confident in the patterns. I'd push for at least 10-12 before moving into design.
The results metrics were defined after the design was done, not before. In future projects I want to define success metrics as part of the brief — so the design is intentionally built toward measurable goals from day one.
